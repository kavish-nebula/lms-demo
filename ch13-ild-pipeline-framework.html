Two Guiding Principles

🧠
Lowest Cognitive Load
Minimize extraneous load. Scaffold intrinsic load. Maximize germane load.

Every design decision must pass the cognitive load test. If a visual, interaction, or content element does not directly serve learning, it is extraneous and must be removed. This principle draws from Sweller's Cognitive Load Theory (Ch 8) and Mayer's 12 multimedia principles.

Operational rules:

  • Zero tolerance for decorative elements in learning materials
  • Maximum 4–5 new concepts per chunk (working memory limit: 4±1 chunks)
  • Worked examples before independent practice (worked-example effect)
  • Guidance fading: fully worked → partially completed → independent
  • Video segments capped at 5–12 minutes
🎯
Maximum Learning
Evidence-based interventions. Measurable outcomes. Research-grounded design.

Every learning activity must be traceable to an evidence-based principle with a documented effect size. This principle draws from the ILD Master Document's entire research foundation (Ch 00–12).

Operational rules:

  • Every objective mapped to a specific Bloom's level and knowledge type
  • Spaced repetition scheduled for all critical content (d = 0.50–0.70)
  • Retrieval practice embedded every 3–5 minutes (d = 0.65)
  • Formative assessment density maximized (d = 0.40–0.70)
  • ARCS motivation check on every sub-module
  • Authentic assessment for higher-order objectives (Bloom's Apply+)

Pipeline Roles
Four core roles. Every task in the pipeline maps to exactly one of these.

Subject Matter Expert (SME)

Owns: Content accuracy, authenticity, and domain validity.

Skills required:

  • Deep domain expertise in the course subject
  • Ability to articulate learning objectives at multiple Bloom's levels
  • Knowledge of real-world performance standards in the domain
  • Willingness to review and provide structured feedback

Time commitment: Heavy in Stages 1 & 3, moderate in Stage 4 (task loop), light in Stage 6.

Typical profile: Industry practitioner, academic faculty, senior engineer, clinical educator.

Pipeline Lead

Owns: Pipeline flow, quality gate decisions, timeline, and escalation resolution.

Skills required:

  • Project management (deadline tracking, blocker resolution)
  • Basic instructional design literacy (can read ILD output and spot obvious gaps)
  • Familiarity with the pipeline stages and quality gate criteria
  • Authority to enforce gate pass/fail decisions

Time commitment: Steady throughout. Lightweight but constant — like a scrum master.

Typical profile: Project manager, program coordinator, senior instructional designer.

Content Creator

Owns: Producing learning assets — videos, interactives, assessments, simulations.

Skills required:

  • Proficiency in 2–3 authoring tools from the Toolset Library (Section 7)
  • Ability to follow Mayer's principles without needing theoretical explanation
  • Visual design sensibility (layout, typography, color)
  • Technical ability to produce accessible content (captions, alt text, keyboard navigation)

Time commitment: Heaviest in Stage 4. Mostly absent from Stages 1–3 and 5–6.

Typical profile: E-learning developer, multimedia designer, video producer, interactive developer.

ILD Reviewer

Owns: Instructional design quality — the Stage 5 ILD QC rubric.

Skills required:

  • Strong instructional design background (ID models, learning theories, taxonomies)
  • Working knowledge of cognitive science principles (Mayer, CLT, spaced repetition)
  • Assessment design expertise (formative vs. summative, authentic assessment, SOLO)
  • Ability to evaluate against the 9-dimension ILD QC rubric objectively

Time commitment: Concentrated in Stage 5. May consult during Stage 2 if AI output needs interpretation.

Typical profile: Senior instructional designer, learning experience designer, educational researcher.

1.2 SME Onboarding Guide
Quick-reference for writing effective content inputs.

Writing Objectives at Bloom's Levels

  • Start with verbs. "The learner will be able to design..." not "The learner will understand..."
  • One verb per objective. "Solve quadratic equations" not "Understand and solve quadratic equations"
  • Make it measurable. Can you observe whether the learner achieved it?
  • Aim for variety. If all objectives are "Remember" or "Understand," the course will be passive. Include at least one Apply or higher.

Identifying Cognitive Load "Danger Zones"

  • High element interactivity: Topics requiring learners to hold 5+ new concepts in mind simultaneously (e.g., multi-step procedures, systems with many interacting parts)
  • Expertise reversal risk: Content that is natural for you as an expert but overwhelming for a novice. Flag topics where you might skip steps unconsciously.
  • Prerequisite chains: Mark where Topic B absolutely requires Topic A to be mastered first. These need mastery gates.

Op 1
Objective Classification & Gap Analysis
Map objectives to Bloom's levels and knowledge types. Identify gaps in higher-order thinking.

The AI maps each SME-stated objective to Bloom's Revised Taxonomy (6 cognitive process levels × 4 knowledge types = 24 possible objective types). It then identifies:

  • Cognitive gaps: Are Analyze, Evaluate, or Create represented? If all objectives cluster at Remember/Understand, the AI flags this (Ch 3 finding: most platforms leave higher levels underserved).
  • Knowledge type variety: Are all four knowledge types (Factual, Conceptual, Procedural, Metacognitive) represented?
  • Constructive alignment check: For each objective, does the provided content actually support it? (Ch 4: Biggs' Constructive Alignment)
Research anchor: Bloom's Revised Taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001) — Chapter 3. ~90% of instructional designers use Bloom's. The 4×6 matrix ensures no objective type is overlooked.

Op 5
Task Design
Practice, application, and extension tasks with evidence-based difficulty calibration.

For each sub-module, the AI generates three tiers of tasks:

Practice Tasks
Guided + unguided
  • Follow worked-example pattern
  • Guidance fading: fully worked → partial → independent
  • Immediate feedback on every submission
Application Tasks
Real-world scenarios
  • Authentic contexts from SME's domain
  • Require integration of multiple concepts
  • Open-ended with rubric-based evaluation
Extension Tasks
For advanced learners
  • Transfer to new contexts
  • Higher Bloom's levels (Analyze/Evaluate/Create)
  • Self-directed with minimal scaffolding

Evidence-Based Task Design Rules

  • Interleaving: Mix problem types within practice sessions, not blocked by category (Ch 8: d = 0.42, 43% better delayed performance)
  • Retrieval practice: Tasks require active recall, not recognition or re-reading (Ch 8: d = 0.65)
  • Desirable difficulties: Calibrate difficulty to be challenging but not frustrating (Ch 8: d = 0.50–0.70 combined)
  • Guidance fading: Fully worked → partially completed → independent (CLT worked-example effect)
  • Authentic context: Tasks reflect real-world professional situations from SME's domain (Ch 6: authentic assessment)

  • Interactive exercises: Immediate feedback within 2 seconds (behaviorist reinforcement, Ch 2)

5.1.1 Constructive Alignment Ch 4: Biggs

ScoreWhat It Looks Like
0 — Not MetObjectives, content, and assessments are misaligned. There are orphan objectives (stated but never taught or tested) or orphan assessments (testing something never taught). No traceable path from objective → content → assessment.

2 — MetEvery stated objective has (a) corresponding content that teaches it, (b) at least one learning activity that practices it, and (c) at least one assessment item that measures it. No orphan elements. Alignment is traceable.
3 — ExemplaryMet criteria PLUS: alignment is explicitly documented (each objective is annotated with its content source, activity, and assessment item). 5.1.2 Bloom's Coverage Ch 3: Bloom's

ScoreWhat It Looks Like
0 — Not MetAll objectives and assessments cluster at Remember/Understand only. No higher-order thinking is required. The "Bloom's Gap" identified in Ch 3 — most platforms leave Analyze/Evaluate/Create underserved.
1 — Partially MetAt least 3 Bloom's levels are targeted, but the module doesn't assess all of them. For example, "Analyze" is in the objectives but assessments only test at Remember–Apply.
2 — MetAll Bloom's levels stated in the objectives have at least one corresponding assessment item. At least one Apply-level or higher objective exists. No level is claimed but untested.
3 — ExemplaryMet criteria PLUS: all 4 knowledge types (Factual, Conceptual, Procedural, Metacognitive) are represented across objectives. Assessment items are mapped to specific cells in the 4×6 Bloom's matrix. At least one Create-level task exists.

5.1.3 Cognitive Load Management Ch 8: CLT

ScoreWhat It Looks Like
0 — Not MetEvidence of extraneous load that hasn't been eliminated: decorative images, split-attention situations (key info separated from where it's needed), content chunks exceeding 4–5 new concepts, no worked examples before independent practice.
1 — Partially MetExtraneous load is mostly eliminated, but scaffolding is inconsistent. Some high-load topics have worked examples but others jump straight to independent practice. Guidance fading is present in some sections but not all.
2 — MetZero decorative elements. Every high-load topic has a worked example before practice. Guidance fading is implemented (fully worked → partial → independent). Content is chunked to max 4–5 new concepts per segment. Video segments are ≤ 12 minutes.
3 — ExemplaryMet criteria PLUS: cognitive load is explicitly budgeted per sub-module (the Content Creator Brief includes a load classification). Scaffolding is adaptive — learners who struggle get additional worked examples. Pre-training is used for complex topics (key terms introduced before the main lesson).

5.1.4 Multimedia Compliance Ch 8: CTML

ScoreWhat It Looks Like
0 — Not MetMayer's principles are violated: on-screen text repeats narration verbatim (redundancy), corresponding words and pictures are separated (split attention), decorative visuals/audio are present (coherence violation), or content is words-only with no visuals (multimedia violation).

5.1.5 Assessment Quality Ch 6

ScoreWhat It Looks Like
0 — Not MetAssessment is summative-only (one test at the end). No formative checks during learning. Questions only test recall (Remember level). No distractor rationale documented. No authentic assessment for Apply+ objectives.
1 — Partially MetSome formative assessment exists, but not at the recommended density (every 3–5 minutes). Distractors exist but lack documented rationale. Authentic assessment is used for some but not all higher-order objectives.
2 — MetFormative assessments embedded at regular intervals (every 3–5 min of content). 5.1.6 Motivation Design Ch 5

ScoreWhat It Looks Like
0 — Not MetNo ARCS analysis. No attention-capturing hooks. No visible connection to learner goals (relevance). Early tasks are too difficult for beginners (confidence). No sense of progress or achievement (satisfaction). Extrinsic rewards are the only motivator (crowding-out risk).
1 — Partially MetSome ARCS elements are present but not all 4. For example, hooks exist (attention) and tasks are scaffolded (confidence), but no relevance to learner goals is shown and there's no meaningful satisfaction mechanism. "The Dip" at 25–35% is not addressed.
2 — MetAll 4 ARCS components are present in every sub-module: a hook captures attention, content connects to learner goals/reality, scaffolded tasks build confidence, and mastery provides natural satisfaction. SDT needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness) are addressed. "The Dip" zone has a planned intervention.
3 — ExemplaryMet criteria PLUS: autonomy is meaningful (genuine choices in path, pace, or project topics — not fake choices). Relatedness includes community features (peer review, galleries, discussion). Motivation is intrinsic-first — gamification serves pedagogy, not the other way around. EVT expectancy is protected: early experiences guarantee success to prevent the 2.3x disengagement risk.

ScoreWhat It Looks Like

5.1.8 Spaced Retrieval Ch 8

ScoreWhat It Looks Like
0 — Not MetNo review schedule. Concepts are taught once and never revisited. All quizzes test only current-module content. No interleaving. Retrieval practice is not embedded — students can passively re-read or re-watch without active recall.
1 — Partially MetSome review exists but it's ad hoc rather than scheduled. A quiz at the end of a module might include 1–2 questions from prior modules, but there's no systematic spaced review plan. Retrieval practice is present in some but not all sections.
2 — MetA spaced review schedule is documented: Review 1 appears in the next module, Review 2 appears 2 modules later, Review 3 appears 4 modules later (expanding intervals). Interleaving is implemented — review quizzes mix questions from 2+ prior modules. Retrieval practice is embedded (active recall, not recognition). At least 1 spaced review quiz exists per prior module.
3 — ExemplaryMet criteria PLUS: review questions appear in new contexts (far transfer, not just re-testing in the original context). Difficulty increases with each review cycle (Bloom's level escalates). Cumulative mastery gates exist that test across all prior modules. The spacing schedule is informed by learner performance data (items learners struggled with are reviewed more frequently).

5.1.9 Kinesthetic & Psychomotor Design Ch 8.8, Ch 10 VR/AR

Routing Decision Rules
Three rules that govern every gate failure routing decision.

Rule 1: Minimal Scope

Route back to the latest possible stage that can fix the problem. Default is always Stage 4 (content revision). Routing to Stage 2 (AI re-processing) requires Pipeline Lead approval because it may cascade changes to sub-modules that already passed SME QC.

Rule 2: No Silent Bypass

Every re-route is logged. The module's version history (see Section 6.2) must show which gate failed, why, where it was routed, and what changed. No gate failure is ever resolved without a record.

Rule 3: Escalation on Repeat

If the same gate fails twice for the same reason, the Pipeline Lead escalates: convene a joint meeting of all involved roles, identify the root cause, and decide whether the brief, the SME input, or the process itself needs adjustment.
